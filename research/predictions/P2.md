# P2: a unique stable matching does not stop fair blocking-pair dynamics

Committed on 2026-10-11, before any attempt to prove or refute it. The commit that adds this file
is its timestamp. The claim, the pass and fail criteria and the probability below are candidate C
of [candidates.md](candidates.md), copied unchanged; they are not edited after this commit. The
kernels are frozen ([README.md](README.md)); the test runs against them.

P1 is reserved for the routing candidate (candidate B, reshaped after the literature check below)
and is not committed.

## Claim: A unique stable matching does not stop fair blocking-pair dynamics

**Model.** The marriage market with `n` men and `n` women and complete strict preferences:
- State: a matching (partial allowed).
- Labels: the `n^2` pairs `(m, w)`.
- Update: `u_(m,w) mu` matches `m` with `w` if `(m, w)` blocks `mu`, meaning each is single or
  prefers the other to the current partner. Their former partners become single. Otherwise the
  update leaves `mu` unchanged. Updates are deterministic.
- Fair schedule: every pair named infinitely often, naming a non-blocking pair being a no-op step,
  as in `DistributedCycles.Fair`.
- With complete lists, the states fixed by every label are exactly the stable matchings.

**Claim (formal).** There are `n <= 4`, preferences with exactly one stable matching `mu*`, and a
start matching `mu0` such that `BadLasso Stable mu0` holds. That is, a pair-word `p` reaches a
matching `mu`, and a nonempty pair-word `q` that names every one of the `n^2` pairs returns `mu` to
itself, with some loop state unstable. Hence fair settlement fails from `mu0`
(`bad_lasso_refutes`), although the B-solution is unique.

**Plain sentence.** In the classical stable marriage market, a unique stable matching does not
prevent blocking-pair dynamics from cycling forever, even when every pair gets infinitely many
chances to block, and this already happens with at most four men and four women.

**Derivation from the kernels.**
- `flip2_unique_solution_livelock`: a unique B-solution does not give fair settlement.
  `q1_unique_iff`: it does when the repair is monotone with the step laws.
- `singleton_iff_solution` and `C2_loops`: the singleton fair recurrent classes are the B-solutions,
  here the stable matchings, and settlement fails exactly through a non-singleton fair loop.
- The step the kernels suggest but do not take. The pair update is not monotone in any order that
  makes `mu*` an extremal fixed point: one agent trades up, a former partner drops to single. So the
  system sits with flip2, not with `q1_unique_iff`, and the kernels predict the flip2 failure here.
- What the kernels do not settle is fairness. A pair that blocks at every state of a cycle must be
  resolved on the loop, and the pairs of `mu*` are natural candidates for that. So fairness over all
  `n^2` pairs may force every cycle to escape to `mu*` once the stable matching is unique. Candidate
  B predicts the opposite outcome (uniqueness suffices) for a different class with the same kernel
  pair; together the two test whether the kernels discriminate.

**Why it is not known.** Read or searched:
- Knuth 1976 (blocking-pair resolution can cycle).
- Roth and Vande Vate 1990 (from every matching some path of blocking-pair resolutions reaches a
  stable matching; random resolution converges with probability one).
- Abeledo and Rothblum 1995 (paths to marriage stability).
- Diamantoudi, Miyagawa and Xue 2004 (roommates).
- Kojima and Ünver 2008 (many-to-many).
- Ackermann, Goldberg, Mirrokni, Röglin and Vöcking 2011 (uncoordinated two-sided markets,
  exponential convergence times).
- "Improved paths to stability for the stable marriage problem" (2020) and "Fragile stable
  matchings" (2024), from the search results; titles and abstracts only.

The cycles in this literature are under an adversary that picks which blocking pair to resolve. No
statement was found that combines fairness over all pairs with a unique stable matching. **The
literature risk:** a known cycling example, Knuth's in particular, may already have a unique stable
matching and admit a pair-fair cycle. Check the full text of Knuth 1976 and Ackermann et al. before
choosing.

**Pass.** An explicit instance with `n <= 4`, its unique stable matching and a bad fair lasso, all
mechanized (uniqueness and the lasso by computation; the refutation of settlement by
`bad_lasso_refutes`).

**Fail.** An exhaustive check, up to the symmetries of relabeling men, women and the sides, showing
that for every `n <= 4` and every preference profile with exactly one stable matching there is no
bad fair lasso from any start. The check is to be run by a program reviewed independently, with
`n <= 3` also decided in Coq (state space small: matchings of 3 by 3).

**What a refutation looks like.** The search finds pair-fair cycles only in profiles with several
stable matchings. Every profile with a unique stable matching settles under every pair-fair
schedule, because some pair of `mu*` blocks throughout each candidate cycle and fairness forces it
to be resolved.

**How it could fail.** The fairness requirement over all `n^2` pairs is much stronger than the
adversarial choice used in the cycling literature. Uniqueness of the stable matching puts structure
on preferences (the man-optimal and woman-optimal matchings coincide) that may make some `mu*` pair
block along every cycle.

**Probability that it holds.** About 0.6 for `n <= 4`.

**Size of the test.**
- Refutation side: `n = 3` is small (46,656 profiles, 34 matchings, 9 labels). `n = 4` needs
  symmetry reduction (on the order of 10^8 profiles after reduction, 209 matchings, 16 labels):
  hours of computation.
- Proof side: one instance, about 200 lines to mechanize.

## Literature check before commitment

A second check was made after candidates.md was written, reading full texts where they could be
found. It was a reading check only: no instance was evaluated against the claim.

- **Knuth 1976.** His cycling example uses the "divorce" operation (the two divorced partners marry
  each other, so only perfect matchings occur), not this model, where they become single. Each
  divorce step is two steps of this model, so Knuth's cycles embed here, but fairness over all
  pairs is not preserved by the embedding. Sources: Chen, Skowron and Sorge, arXiv 1906.12274;
  Rudov, arXiv 2403.12183.
- **Roth and Vande Vate 1990** (as quoted by Ackermann et al., Rudov, and Chen et al.): in this
  model some path of blocking-pair resolutions reaches a stable matching from every matching, and
  random resolution with probabilities bounded below converges almost surely. This says nothing
  about deterministic pair-fair schedules.
- **Nearest instances.** Tamura 1993 (J. Combin. Theory Ser. A 62), reproduced as Example 2 in Chen
  et al.: a 4 by 4 market and a start from which no divorce sequence reaches stability. Ackermann,
  Goldberg, Mirrokni, Röglin and Vöcking, SIAM J. Comput. 2011, Thm 3: a 3 by 3 best-response
  cycle. No source states that either market has a unique stable matching, or checks fairness over
  all pairs.
- **No statement found** combining a unique stable matching with fairness over all pairs.
- Not read in full: Knuth's book (1976; English translation 1997), Roth and Vande Vate 1990, Tamura
  1993, Abeledo and Rothblum 1995, Diamantoudi, Miyagawa and Xue 2004, Kojima and Ünver 2008.

## Test plan

1. Check the two nearest published instances (Tamura's 4 by 4, Ackermann et al.'s 3 by 3) for
   uniqueness of the stable matching and for a pair-fair cycle. A loop is pair-fair in this model
   exactly when every pair is either resolved on the loop or not blocking at some state of it. If
   either instance passes, mechanize it (pass).
2. Otherwise run the exhaustive check of the Fail criterion for `n <= 3`, in Coq, and for `n = 4`
   by a program reviewed independently, with symmetry reduction.
3. Record the outcome here, whatever it is, with the instance or the search and its code.

## Outcome

**PASS** (tested 2026-10-11, scored against the Pass criterion above, kernels frozen). The claim
holds already at `n = 3`.

**The instance** (0-based; label `j = 3m + w` names the pair `(m, w)`):

| | 1st | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|
| m0 | w0 | w1 | w2 |
| m1 | w0 | w1 | w2 |
| m2 | w1 | w2 | w0 |
| w0 | m2 | m0 | m1 |
| w1 | m1 | m2 | m0 |
| w2 | m2 | m0 | m1 |

- The only stable matching is `mu* = {m0-w0, m1-w1, m2-w2}` (man-proposing and woman-proposing
  Gale-Shapley agree).
- From the empty matching, `p = [(m0,w2), (m2,w0)]` reaches `x = {m0-w2, m2-w0}`.
- The loop word `q = [0, 2, 5, 7, 1, 8, 4, 3, 6]` names each of the 9 pairs exactly once and
  returns to `x`. Its real moves are `x -> {m0-w2, m2-w1} -> {m0-w2, m1-w1} -> {m0-w2, m1-w0} -> x`
  (pairs 7, 4, 3, 6). The other five labels are no-ops: 0, 2, 5 at `x`, and 1, 8 at
  `{m0-w2, m2-w1}`. Every loop state is unstable.
- So the periodic round-robin schedule `p q q q ...` never settles, although the stable matching
  is unique. The "what a refutation looks like" mechanism fails here: the `mu*` pair `(m0, w0)`
  does not block at `x` (w0 holds m2, whom she prefers), so fairness does not force it.

**Mechanized** in [coq/StableMatchingP2.v](../../coq/StableMatchingP2.v), axiom-free, through the
frozen kernels:
- `stable_iff_no_blocking` (generic `n`): `DistributedCycles.Stable` for this update is "no
  blocking pair".
- `p2_wellformed`: complete strict preferences. `p2_unique_stable`: for every matching `mu`,
  `Stable mu <-> mu = mu*` (by computation over all candidate lists).
- `p2_bad_lasso`: `BadLasso Stable` from the empty matching. `p2_loop_unstable`: every loop state
  is unstable. `p2_round_robin`: the loop word is a permutation of the 9 labels.
- `p2_refutes_settlement`: `~ FairSettles` and `~ FairSettlesNN` from the empty matching, by
  `CanonicalRecurrence.bad_lasso_refutes`.
- `p2_pass`: the committed claim as one statement (`n <= 4`, well-formed preferences, a unique
  stable matching, a start matching with a bad fair lasso and no fair settlement).

**How it was found** (test plan steps 1 and 2; programs in [p2/](p2/)):
- Step 1, published instances. Ackermann et al. (2011, Thm 3, 3 by 3): 2 stable matchings, and a
  pair-fair strongly connected set of 30 matchings. Tamura (1993, via Chen et al. Ex. 2, 4 by 4):
  5 stable matchings, a pair-fair strongly connected set of 203 matchings. Both cycle under
  pair-fair schedules, but neither has a unique stable matching, so neither decides P2.
- Step 2, exhaustive search. The criterion: a bad fair lasso exists from some start iff some
  strongly connected component of the state graph with an edge has, for every pair, a state where
  the pair does not block or an internal edge labeled by it (exact; see [p2/README.md](p2/README.md)).
- `n = 2`: 16 profiles, 14 with a unique stable matching, none with a fair cycle.
- `n = 3`: 46,656 profiles; 34,080 with a unique stable matching; 14,916 of those have a
  nontrivial strongly connected component; **3,240 have a pair-fair one**. The Python reference
  (all profiles, about 15 s) and the C search with symmetry reduction give the same counts.
- `n = 4`: 24^8 = 110,075,314,176 profiles, covered through 762 men's-profile representatives
  (252,813,312 profiles analysed; 781 s wall clock on 8 processes, 5,144 CPU seconds).
  65,867,261,184 have a unique stable matching; 58,925,668,608 of those have a nontrivial
  strongly connected component; **17,655,840,000 have a pair-fair one** (about 27%).
  Counts in [p2/results_n4.txt](p2/results_n4.txt).
- In every profile with two or more stable matchings (`n = 2, 3, 4`) some pair-fair cycle exists.
- Checks: [p2/test_p2.py](p2/test_p2.py) reproduces Ackermann et al.'s printed cycle as a
  blocking-pair cycle, checks uniqueness against Gale-Shapley on all `n = 3` profiles, and compares
  the C program with the Python reference (full `n = 2, 3`; 400 random `n = 4` profiles). The
  instance was also checked by a separate script written independently of these programs.

**Robustness beyond the committed claim** (not part of the scored claim, which uses
`DistributedCycles.Fair`). The round-robin loop is fair in the committed sense but not strongly
fair: the pair `(m0, w0)` blocks at `{m0-w2, m1-w0}` (and at two other loop states) and is never
resolved on the loop. The instance also has strongly fair cycles: an Emerson-Lei check
(`strong_fair_sets` in [p2/p2ref.py](p2/p2ref.py)) finds two sets of unstable matchings, of 11
and 17 states, in which every pair that blocks somewhere in the set is resolved by an edge inside
it. For `n = 3` the strongly fair counts equal the pair-fair counts: 3,240 profiles with a unique
stable matching, and all 12,576 with several ([p2/p2ref_strong.py](p2/p2ref_strong.py)).

The probability recorded at commitment was about 0.6.
